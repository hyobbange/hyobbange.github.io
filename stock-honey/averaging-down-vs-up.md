# Averaging Down vs Averaging Up

When you buy more of a stock you already own, buying after the price has fallen is **averaging down**, and buying after it has risen is **averaging up**. Averaging down lowers your average price; averaging up raises it. Either way you put in more money, so future price moves affect you more.

| | Averaging down | Averaging up |
|---|---|---|
| When you buy | Price now < your average | Price now > your average |
| Average price | Goes down | Goes up |
| Money invested | Increases | Increases |

Both use the same average price formula.

<p class="formula">New average = (your average × your shares + buy price × shares bought) ÷ (your shares + shares bought)</p>

The new average always lies **between your old average and the buy price**. Buy cheaper and it goes down; buy higher and it goes up. The more you buy, the closer it gets to the buy price.

## Comparing with the same stock

You hold 40 shares with an average price of 58,100 (2,324,000 invested). Let's buy 30 more shares in two different situations. Amounts are in the same currency as the share price.

| | Averaging down | Averaging up |
|---|---|---|
| Price now | 52,000 (about 10.5% below your average) | 64,000 (about 10.2% above your average) |
| Average after buying 30 | **55,485.71** | **60,628.57** |
| Money invested | 3,884,000 | 4,244,000 |
| Profit or loss at today's price | −244,000 (same as before buying) | +236,000 (same as before buying) |
| Rate of return | −10.50% → **−6.28%** | +10.15% → **+5.56%** |

The key point: **buying more at today's price does not change your profit or loss right away.** The rate of return changes only because the amount invested got bigger. What changes is how much future price moves affect you.

### Averaging down: break even comes sooner

- Before buying: the price must rise **+11.73%**, from 52,000 to 58,100, to break even
- After buying: a rise of **+6.70%**, to 55,485.71, is enough

But if the price keeps falling, the loss grows too. If it drops another 10% from 52,000 to 46,800:

- Before buying: (46,800 − 58,100) × 40 = **−452,000**
- After buying: (46,800 − 55,485.71) × 70 = **−608,000**

### Averaging up: more gain on the way up, less cushion

- Before buying: you stay in profit until the price falls below 58,100 (**−9.22%** from now)
- After buying: you are at a loss once it falls below 60,628.57 (**−5.27%** from now)

If the price drops 10% from 64,000 to 57,600:

- Before buying: (57,600 − 58,100) × 40 = **−20,000**
- After buying: (57,600 − 60,628.57) × 70 = **−212,000**

In both cases, buying more makes price moves matter more. You gain more if it rises and lose more if it falls.

## Things to keep in mind

- **A lower average does not mean a smaller loss.** As the example shows, profit or loss at today's price stays the same.
- **The money invested grows too.** Look at the larger amount, not just the average price.
- **The effect shrinks as you buy more.** Pulling your average close to the buy price takes rapidly more shares. The calculator's "Averaging down list" shows this.
- **Fees and taxes are not included.** Real amounts can be slightly different.

This page only shows the math. Whether to average down or up is your own decision.

## FAQ

### Can averaging up ever lower my average price?

No. Buying above your average always raises it.
